- I start with the problem: understanding the context, observing what feels easy or frustrating, and asking why something works the way it does before jumping into a screen or feature.
- I think in systems. A product sits within workflows, existing tools, conventions and people's mental models. I look for how those pieces connect and how the experience can fit in without unnecessary friction.
- My process: understand the problem and the people experiencing it, map the workflow, look for friction and edge cases, explore directions, make ideas tangible through flows, prototypes or working components, get feedback, test assumptions where possible, and iterate. It changes with the problem; it isn't a fixed checklist.
- I don't expect every project to have all the answers at the start. I test an idea, find what doesn't work, revisit decisions and improve it one iteration at a time.
- Code isn't about becoming an engineer for me. It's a way to become a sharper designer: to move beyond what's easy to mock up, understand real product constraints, prototype interactions and talk with engineers in the same language.
- I use code throughout the process, from quick demos and experiments to components and end-to-end prototypes. I'm comfortable exploring a codebase and connecting design decisions to how something is built.
- I don't think every designer needs to code. I work in codebases and can create or review pull requests, but I don't define my work by an engineering title.
- Designing for AI: I start with the user's task, not the fact that AI is involved. I think about where AI genuinely helps, what the user needs to understand or control, how uncertainty and errors are handled, and what happens when the AI gets something wrong. I want AI to fit naturally into an existing workflow, not be added just because it's possible.
- With engineers and product managers: I bring them into the problem-solving early. My engineering background helps me understand constraints and discuss implementation, while I keep the user's workflow at the centre. I like working through prototypes and edge cases together rather than treating design as a handoff.
- Feedback and disagreement: I try to understand the reason behind feedback instead of defending the first version. I ask questions, look at the problem and constraints together, and explore alternatives. I'm comfortable changing direction when the evidence or reasoning points somewhere better.
- A mistake I learned from, while building System: some decisions that seem reasonable in isolation become awkward when components, documentation and implementation have to work together. I've learned to revisit foundations, test how pieces connect, and treat maintenance as part of the design work.
- My weakness: I'm still early in my design career, and I'm learning how to make good decisions across the full life of a product, not just the first version. Maintaining System has made me more aware of how much iteration, documentation and consistency matter. I treat those gaps as things to work on.
- Owning a feature end to end: I'm interested in taking a problem from understanding the workflow through exploration, prototyping, feedback and implementation. End-to-end ownership depends on the scope and team, so I'd rather talk through the specific expectations than claim experience I can't back up.
- Why hire me: I can't fairly compare myself with candidates I haven't met. What I bring is curiosity about complex problems, a product-design perspective, an engineering background that helps me understand implementation, and a willingness to prototype, learn and iterate. I'd hope to show that through the work.
